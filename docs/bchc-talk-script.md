**How to read it.** Each slide has three parts:
- **On screen:** the title, then the story lines that rise under the picture. Someone watching with no speaker should get the slide from these alone.
- **They see:** what the animation shows. One click plays the whole slide. Slide 19 takes two clicks, and so does slide 27.
- **You say:** what the speaker says. It adds detail, and the slide doesn't depend on it.

**Colour cues** in They see use role names from the Austin Public Health brand: the "a person approves" mark, the "passed" tick, the catalog's colour and the process's colour. Those quoted role names are styles, not words on screen. Every other quoted phrase in They see is a label on screen.

**The story in six moves:**
1. Here's a tool you can use today: the catalog.
2. We built it in three working sessions with our agentic development process.
3. Here's how that process works, and how we've refined it. It's a general way to build tools, data pipelines and more.
4. When every city shares in the catalog and builds with a process like ours, everyone's toolset grows.
5. Here's what we're sharing to make that possible.
6. Here's how your department starts.

Part 2 (slides 11, 11a, 12 and 13) is the bridge between moves 2 and 3: why every department needs this.

The process and the catalog are two separate things. The process built the catalog, and we're sharing both.

## Part 1. The catalog (about 8 minutes)

**On screen:** Using AI in Public Health Practice. Big Cities Health Coalition, Data Modernization Work Group. Austin Public Health.
- AI in public health is moving from chatbots inside tools to agents that build the tools.
- We built the coalition's use case catalog that way, and we'll show how your department can too.

**They see:** The title and credit.

**You say:** Thanks for having us. This is launch week for something we've been working on with the coalition, so if you haven't heard about it yet, I'll start there: a new tool that came out of a collaboration between Austin Public Health and the Big Cities Health Coalition, and every department here can use it today. This whole conference is about big cities coming together and getting things done, and this is one example of that. Most of the AI reaching our tools today is features inside products; I'll show you agents that build the tools themselves. Then I'll show you the agentic development process we used to build it in about three working sessions, and how we plan to share that process with you, so your department can build its own tools the same way.

## Part 2. Why this matters (about 5 minutes)

### 11. The problem we all share
**On screen:** For most health departments, getting a new tool takes months or years and costs too much.
- Think about the last tool your team asked for.
- It can take months or years, the answer can still be no, and every later change needs a new quote.

**They see:** A request card, "We need a tool", sits on the left. Beside it, a wall calendar has a page for each month, its name at the top: "Jan", "Feb", "Mar", "Apr", "May", "Jun", "Jul", "Aug", "Sep", "Oct", over the calendar's own "Nov". The pages turn while the card waits, each a little faster than the last, and "Months or years" appears. Quotes arrive one at a time, each labelled "New quote", and a stack of dollar signs beside the card grows taller with each one as another page turns, faster again. A "No" stamp swings over the card and lifts: the answer can still be no. Then a change request arrives, labelled "Change request", and it starts again: another "New quote", more dollar signs, and the last pages flip fastest of all, until the calendar shows November. All in grey.

**You say:** You wrote requirements, went through procurement, waited for a vendor and waited for setup. Sometimes the answer after all that was no. By the time the tool arrived the program had changed. Meanwhile your staff kept the work going in spreadsheets.

### 11a. The tools gap
**On screen:** Your tools are gaining AI features, but the tools you still need aren't getting built.
- An AI feature inside a tool you already use can be useful, though it often raises the price.
- It doesn't close the tools gap: the pipeline, analysis tool or dashboard you've been waiting for.

**They see:** A product card on the left, labelled "A tool you already have", with its price under it as a row of dollar signs. An AI badge, labelled "AI", slides across and settles inside the card, and the price grows another row of dollar signs. On the right, three dashed outlines wait, labelled "Data pipeline", "Analysis tool" and "Dashboard". The card keeps its badge; the outlines stay empty. All in grey.

**You say:** Many of the tools you already pay for are adding AI features this year. Some of those features are useful. But the price often goes up, and the tools your team has been asking for still aren't there.

### 12. AI that builds the tool
**On screen:** To close the tools gap, have AI agents build the tools you need.
- AI inside a tool helps with the work that tool was built for.
- With agentic development, your staff describe what they need, and AI agents build the whole tool.

**They see:** An app outline with a chat bubble inside: "AI inside a tool". A prohibition sign is drawn over it. Then the picture itself shrinks and travels to the left, carrying the sign, and stays there, faded, leaving the empty outline in place; the first story line leaves the screen. A person, labelled "Program staff", describes what they need. An AI agent, labelled "AI agent", outside the outline fills it with building blocks: "AI that builds the tool". The program staff approve the finished tool with the "a person approves" mark.

**You say:** So how do you get the tools that aren't coming? Have AI agents build them. The people who know the work, your epidemiologists and program staff, describe what they need. Agents plan it, build it, test it and review each other's work.

### 13. You own it
**On screen:** When your agents build the tool, you own it.
- The code lives in your own GitHub account, with every change and every approval on record.
- When your program changes, you describe the change, agents make it and someone on your team approves it.

**They see:** The app with "Yours" drawn around it. A history line underneath, labelled "Every change and every approval on record", gains a new approval mark.

**You say:** Because your agents built it, the tool is yours. When the program changes next month, you don't wait for a new quote.

## Part 3. How our agentic development process works (about 12 minutes)

### 14. The process
**On screen:** We build tools, data pipelines and dashboards with our agentic development process.
- AI agents now do most of the building and testing.

**They see:** Three steps appear in a row, one at a time: People decide what to build. AI agents build and test it. A person approves the release. The first and last steps show people, and the last carries the "a person approves" mark. The middle step shows agents at work in the process's colour.

**You say:** This is the process itself. We use it for websites, and also for data pipelines, dashboards and other technical work. People decide what to build, AI agents build it and test it, and a person approves every release. It grew out of rapid application development, where the people who'll use a tool check it after each short build cycle. In Austin we've been refining it for years, and it keeps getting more agentic.

### 18. Monday meeting, Friday demo
**On screen:** We meet on Monday and show a working prototype on Friday.
- On Tuesday, the people who need the tool check the plan the agents wrote.
- On Friday, those same people click through it and tell us what to change.

**They see:** A desk calendar shows one week, Monday to Friday, above a single app sketch. Each day the calendar page turns, and the sketch fills in a little more. Thursday: the agents test it. Friday: the sketch is a working prototype, and a cursor clicks through it.

**You say:** On Monday we listen to the people who'll use the tool. Then the agents turn our notes into requirements and a plan, and on Tuesday the people from Monday tell us what's wrong with it. On Wednesday and Thursday the agents build and test. If we got something wrong, we find out within the week.

### 14a. What the lead agents read first
**On screen:** Before any building starts, our lead agents read everything we've set up.
- They read notes from past projects, rules on what agents may do, and our written skills.
- These lead agents, called orchestrators, plan the work and hand it out.

**They see:** The lead agents, labelled "Lead agents", sit at a desk. Six folders land on it one at a time, each with its label on the spine: "Notes from past projects", "Rules on what agents may and may not do", "Skills, written instructions for agents", "Our technology and how we want it built", "Tested modules", "Design system". The design system and tested modules folders stay on top, because the next two slides open them.

**You say:** Most of our refinements live here. The notes are our memory layer. The rules are a permissions system that controls what agents may and may not do. The skills give them our way of working. Add the design system, the modules and our tech stack, and the agents start from everything we've learned.

### 15. The design system
**On screen:** Agents start from our design system.
- It sets the colors, fonts and buttons, so every tool looks and feels the same.
- Agents follow our skills to apply it to each tool and check the result.

**They see:** A strip of the design system draws across the top: swatches in the City's colors, the same indigo and green as the Austin Public Health logo, then fonts and buttons. **You say:** We always start from the design system: the City's colors, type and layouts, and ready-made components. A form, a dashboard and a map built months apart still look like they belong together.

## Part 4. Sharing and collaboration (about 8 minutes)

The point of this part is to get people excited about sharing. Every tool a department shares grows the pool that all of us, and all our agents, can build from. One each from three departments gives every department three tools. The agents do the heavy lifting of adapting them.

### 23. Share one tool, get them all
**On screen:** When three departments each share one tool, all three get three tools.
- The catalog holds what they share, and each department takes what it needs.
- Each department's own AI agents do the heavy lifting of adapting each tool to fit.

**They see:** Three departments stand along the bottom, labelled Department A, Department B and Department C. Each holds one tool with its own icon and name: a "Clinic finder" with a map pin, a "Dashboard" with a bar chart, and a "Data pipeline" with joined pipes. Each tool sits at the foot of a stack of three places. Above them, a still of the catalog's card grid, labelled "The catalog", is cropped to three empty slots, so no other entry can be read. One by one, each department sends its tool up into a slot, where it lands as a card in the catalog's colour. Then copies flow back down, one at a time, and no card ever crosses another. Each copy waits beside that department's own agents while they adapt it, then drops into the stack with a dashed corner labelled "Adapted". The agents are labelled once, "AI agents". Each department now holds all three tools, its own at the foot, told apart by icon and name, and the count under the catalog reads "3 tools shared, 3 tools each". No other slide shows departments under the catalog.

**You say:** This is what I'd like you to get excited about. You share one thing, you share one thing, you share one thing, and now as a group we have three tools and every one of us has all three. The catalog is where we share. The process is how each of us takes something and makes it ours. The more we share, the bigger the pool we and our agents can build from.

### 24. When every member shares
**On screen:** If every member shares one, every member gets all of them, and the catalog keeps growing.
- Share the source code too when you can, so others can build from it.
- Your agents adapt the ones you need to fit your department.

**They see:** The view starts close on one outline labelled "Your department", holding a single tile labelled "Yours": the use case you shared. Tiles from other members start to land in it, in the catalog's colour: one, then two at once, then four, then too many to follow. The outline packs full and widens to make room, and new tiles keep landing at its edges as the slide ends. As each tile lands, a dashed corner shows your agents adapting it. No number appears anywhere.

**You say:** It's the module idea at coalition scale. Every member shares one use case, and every member gets all of them, with AI tailoring each one to each department. We stop spending that money, and we stop waiting months or years to be turned down. Each of us can also build new modules and post them, so the pool keeps growing. We start the work and get it done.
